Verifying AI Answers
at Work

A guide for engineering teams on when to draft with AI, when to check a claim, and when to leave an answer unresolved.

Professor Predict · AI Training Interaction

Introduction

In Assignment 3.3, I completed two rounds of Professor Predict, an interactive exercise about how large language models generate responses and where those responses need checking. The exercise asked me to supply plausible answers myself. It gave me a concrete way to explain why an answer can sound convincing even when there is no evidence for it.

Description

The standalone Word guide adapts examples from my two completion summaries into a decision table and a checklist. It is written for engineering teams that use AI to draft messages or summarize information and need a quick way to decide what to verify before sharing.

Objective

Distinguish tasks where an AI draft is useful from tasks that depend on verified facts. Give team leads a repeatable way to identify claims, check the evidence and handle gaps.

Process

  1. Reviewed the two completion summaries for decisions that showed different kinds of risk, including an invented answer for the fictional country of Velmora and a misclassification of current stock prices.
  2. Separated what I did in the exercise from the workplace actions the guide proposes. I used incident status as a workplace example of a fact that can go stale.
  3. Used NIST’s discussion of plausible but inaccurate AI outputs as background and organized the guide around the checks a reader can perform before sharing a claim.

Tools and Technologies Used

Professor Predict provided the learning interaction. The completion summaries supplied the exercise evidence. I used AI-assisted editing and Word-compatible authoring to prepare the guide for engineering teams. The work applies verification reasoning to a learning exercise and proposes checks a team could use in practice.

Value Proposition

Unique Value

The guide turns two decisions I made during the exercise into different checks. An unsupported premise calls for context about the entity itself, while a time-sensitive fact calls for a current source and a timestamp.

Relevance

An engineering manager can use the guide when onboarding staff to AI tools or reviewing an AI-assisted incident update. It shows how I translate a model limitation into a concrete review step, a skill I can apply when helping teams adopt AI reliably.
